> "Therefore, the value comes from what is there, But the use comes from what is not there."  
> – Tao Te Ching, Chapter 11 (Stefan Stenudd translation)

The [previous article](/way/posts/getting-somewhere/) talked about the power of using 'better' as a direction, not a demand. When you know your direction and can focus on that, it all fits. But what if you don't have that focus?

## The curse of many demands

All too often a team ends up with too many demands. A team might hold a big pile of systems and software, all needing care and attention. Perhaps they work in a high pressure area that serves and supports many features and product flows. A team might be directed by a diverse steering committee, bringing a broad set of opinions and directions. It might be simply that the team has been super successful. Too much to do is the curse of a successful team.

There are many reasons why separating what your team might do and what they must do becomes tricksy. The result is the same: direction drifts on the winds of change, operating on an urgency of this week's hot-topic and vibes. And that is the death of long-term team and stakeholder happiness.

Finding stability of direction can be difficult. Focus and work can bring moments of clarity and direction. But that can get crowded out again by options, and recycled demands.

In this situation you, as a lead, need to find out what your team is here for. The one thing this team can deliver that everyone wants to see. Then articulate it, again and again. To do this you need headspace and tools to help clear the noise.

## Story: Finding the mission

A team operated and maintained a high-throughput sub-system. This sat at the centre of many flows through the company's key product. It performed high-scale ingestion of data from many domains, driving other features and different forms of exporting and reporting.

It sat in a monolith, in a design that the business had outgrown. This made it the most critical piece in the system but a tricky piece too. Many teams across the business had needs in the monolith, but this team's entities were the GOATs of the monolith. Quality, uptime and overall maintenance fell to this team.

That led to a really broad brief. Production issues, upgrades, customer issues, data issues, new behaviours and enhancement demands from many product teams.

Worse, it divided the team. The broad set of demands allowed the individuals to deeply focus on the parts that they were best skilled at, solving individual problems and only sometimes coming together to build a thing, all unhappy but entrenched. How could they say no to a request, if they didn't know what they were here to do?

I was the tech lead of this team, and we had solved some big issues: a way out of the monolith for the business, and a more safely-scaled monolith. But it was hard to steer the ship with such ownership and a pressure to right the stability issues.

If these people were to be a team, they needed to find focus. And operate towards a goal that came from their experiences and knowledge.

### How the team found their focus

Inspired by [Rumelt's "Good Strategy Bad Strategy"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Strategy_Bad_Strategy), I ran a set of structured discussion sessions. We worked to answer three questions:

1. **A Diagnosis: *Where are we now?***  
   The current situation, both good and bad, focused on reality, not blame, nor history.

2. **Our Mission: *What are we here for?***  
   Answering the really hard questions: What are the things only we can do? Of those, what's most valuable?

3. **Action orientation: *How do we move forward?***  
   Guardrails to guide us and keep us safe and coherent actions to move us forward. What's within reach? What moves us closer to the mission's goal?

It turned out the key part was the writing (and sharing) of the mission statement. Each team member wrote their own version and brought it to the group, and they were surprisingly similar. None of the team saw value in maintaining the status quo. They wanted to restructure, giving connected teams freedom to move and own their own domains. They wanted to scale and shape the technologies so they were suitable for how the business wanted to grow and be. Everything else was either firefighting — important but needed time-bounding — or noise that distracted from the enabling change only they could deliver.

### Joined up and looking forward

After the sessions, the team was there in a way it was not before. There was a common language and focus when talking about what work the team should do, and strong agreement on what work was valuable and what was noise. They could all present and defend the key work from distractions. Saying no and making time to plan became easier.

The team couldn't say no to all general maintenance, but they did give it a budget. Each team request was met with the question: *how does this help with scale or better ownership?*

## Principle: A team needs space to hold what is most important

The workshop gave the team a pause, and asked them for their truth. That truth became a mission, and the mission cleared the space to work in.

Before the workshop-driven reset, the team was always full. Owning a hundred things, they could easily be given another — after all, what's one more? Owning a clear goal meant not only could they say no, but non-aligned work was less likely to come their way. Clearing away noise and keeping it away.

Owning everything also lets a team hide from each other. With so much to do, collaborating, mobbing, only happens in a crisis. Firefighting, not fireproofing. Instead everyone can get heads down in their area of expertise. When that becomes normal, sharing, teaching, can quietly disappear. No one has to agree. Everyone can, to an extent, do as they please, whilst the key problem does not get solved.

A mission that holds one thing creates space to think, space to be a team and solve the problem no one can do alone.

### Empty space is potential

The Tao Te Ching speaks to the value of empty space. A bowl provides value from its space. Similarly, the spokes on a wheel provide the support, but the empty hub is what lets it turn. A void to fill with new ideas and value.

Hold everything and you are full. Hold one thing, and there is room for the wheel to turn. Find and choose the one thing only your team can do. It's what lets you say no to the rest.
