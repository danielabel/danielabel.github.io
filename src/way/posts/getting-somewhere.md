> "Study the hard while it's easy. Do big things while they're small."  
> – Tao Te Ching, Chapter 63 (Ursula K. Le Guin version)

Something new is what building software is about. New product, new features, new scale, new market, new technology. The journey can be joyous and endless; exploring and learning.

It's not always like that though, sometimes the beginnings of a new puzzle or build can feel daunting or dangerous. To do our jobs, we try to tie down the future. Predict, plan, pin-down what might be, so we can build.

Maybe you hold an expectation of knowing the right answers at the start, being in control and super confident of your outcomes. Everyone else seems to, why not me?

Trying to match these ideals and hold perfect insight can cause great exhaustion, when in reality we are marching forward to meet a goal that is far away, that might not exist when we get there. Life's like that.

## Change is inevitable

The very nature of things is to change. The more we fix things in place, the more that change can affect, distract and frustrate us. Worse, the drive to make firm decisions at a point of strong uncertainty and ambiguity can disrupt our clear thinking and stress us out.

I felt this. A lot. I wanted to solve the big problems, invite in new challenges, but I didn't want to get stuck, stressed and make faulty choices.

I tried something new. Instead of solving everything, I chose to take a solid step forward, in the flow of the challenge, acknowledging that I couldn't know all that the future might bring.

Accepting what I could not control, I was freed to do something much better.

## Story: a new kind of team

A new team was formed at an Ed-tech company, to solve the escalating challenge of digital security. A six-month trial had been successful, and with the seconded engineers returning to their teams, two full time engineers were hired for the team.

This brought two new challenges.

The six-month trial had been time-bound, to prove a case for the team. Quick wins and achievable goals were the operating model. With a permanent team, the goalposts shifted. Now the team needed to deliver on the harder, crunchier, long term aims of engineering-led security.

Two new hires at the centre of the team needed to be onboarded. Super-talented, experienced with great can-do. But new to the company and the security mission.

There was a long game to play. A change to company culture. Problems that were big, destinations they might never quite reach. Overwhelming, felt impossible.

### Can't get there from here

Reaching for quick wins felt safe, but that wasn't the game now. We were funded to solve the big-ticket problems that had been identified, so I picked one: vulnerability management.

The aim of product teams managing the vulnerability exposure and risk was a tough nut to crack. It meant removing friction, providing new calls to action, clear measurement and target setting. A mix of new software, governance and culture change — a massive leap for any security team.

We couldn't get there from where we were. We could be reactive and choose to do something within reach. We could attempt to build a plan, but would likely enter analysis paralysis — we didn't know enough: new domain, new team, new hires. We needed to make moves, but they needed to fit what we were there for.

I could see two traps ahead. The first I'd already seen with my seconded engineers. Four or five months in, we had a big review where I had to convince them how much they'd achieved. They were product developers, used to shipping in weeks, and on a novel problem in a new domain they just couldn't see their own progress. I was seeing early signs of the same in the new hires. If the team couldn't hold the long goal, they'd struggle to own it or feel powerful in it.

The second trap was mine. This terrain was new to me too. I can usually see way over the horizon, but here, I wasn't confident I had the answers, and I could easily lead us down blind alleys. We weren't going round in circles, not yet — but I could see that we would.

### The move: horizons

Instead I treated the goal as a direction of travel: as an organisation we needed to get progressively better at managing vulnerabilities. As a security team we needed to learn about that whilst enabling engineering to be better at it. I understood how teams learn, this was something I could be confident about.

I used a method of planning that allows a team to stretch out the solution space into several horizons, to better identify the steps forward we could take. A combination of lean thinking and McKinsey's three horizons.

Vague items were pushed up to Horizon 2, and the much harder to see work pushed to Horizon 3, with spike and data-gathering options identified in Horizon 1 instead. What that got us was a shared view of the best steps we could take now, that would add value **and** provide a better view of our future options. But most importantly we mapped out the work together, puzzling it out as a team, and then presenting it to our customers (the engineers).

### Where it took us

Did this map help us find our way? From that initial map of work, the team delivered on all of Horizon 1 and half of Horizon 2. Some work from Horizon 3 was pulled forward. Was that a win? Absolutely.

Though delivering, we learnt of new work that was important. We got visibility, value and feedback early. We changed how the whole org approached vulnerability management, from teams to the board and then pivoted to the next big goal.

## Principle: Better is a direction, not a constant

It took me a long time to find and recognize 'Better is a direction' as a guiding principle. I spent a lot of my early career looking around for an answer to exploring vs planning. I found partial answers in lean and agile, in [Barry Boehm's Spiral model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_model), and rapid prototyping. These helped.

Then one day I found it, printed almost verbatim in a book. It guided me to surrender to the flow of discovery, to follow the trail, seeking value and new data in short steps. I was free to do a far better, more rewarding, job. I didn't need to break myself to have all the answers.

This principle changes how plans work. Instead of betting everything on arriving at fixed points and aligning, we can build plans that invite learning and assessment. Instead of "are we there yet," the question becomes "are we facing toward better?" Every step taken answers something. Every step is the start of the next question.

Destinations point the way, and guide us forward, until they stop mattering. This provides a looseness that demands focus and tight cycles of assessment, but allows teams to learn and explore whilst bringing home value against a real problem.

In Tao-ish terms that's about flowing forward, accepting the unknowable but acting anyway. Making the hard things simple, before they become too large. Planting seedlings, don't plan monuments.

## Reclaim exploring

We spend a lot of effort reaching towards a place where all things are good. Our codebases are clean and the team is firing on all cylinders. Where we are leaders with all the information we need, standing in the right place to take right action.

Instead we can work out which way is better and take a step in that direction, and another, and another. Reclaim the job of exploring, pick a direction, actively explore, and when that doesn't serve, pick another.
